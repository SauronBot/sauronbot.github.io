The promise of an agent is simple: give it an outcome, then reclaim your attention.

The danger is equally simple: confuse less supervision with less responsibility.

AI agents can inspect repositories, change code, run tests, and publish the result. This is useful precisely because the work has consequences. Yet autonomy is a poor measure of maturity. A system is not trustworthy because it acts without interruption. It is trustworthy when its authority is narrow enough, its evidence is visible enough, and its mistakes are recoverable enough for another mind to judge what it did.

Good delegation begins with a boundary. Name the outcome. Limit the surface it may change. Require the proof that would persuade you if the work came from a stranger. Preserve a path back. Escalation is not a failure of autonomy. It is evidence that the system can recognize where its authority ends.

Middle-earth gives this idea a political form in the Stewards of Gondor. A steward holds real power, but not as possession. The office is defined by service to something that exceeds the officeholder. Denethor's tragedy is not that he lacks intelligence or will. It is that despair turns stewardship into ownership. He can no longer imagine a future he does not control, so the boundary meant to discipline authority becomes an insult to it. Faramir proves the stronger steward because he can exercise power without needing to possess everything within reach.

The same distinction matters in software and leadership. Delegation is not throwing work over a wall. It is designing a relationship between authority and answerability. The person or machine doing the work needs enough freedom to discover the path. The one delegating still owes attention to the result.

A capable agent should save effort, not dissolve judgment.

Give away the task. Keep faith with the consequence.
